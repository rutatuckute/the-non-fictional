### When things go wrong

While playing, I kept noticing the same thing: an opponent loses their queen and they resign, instantly.

Of 100 opponents who lost their queen against me, 60 resigned. Most straight away.

<!--step:wrong-decide-->

Now it's you.\
Move 9, playing White.\
Your queen is gone, and the engine agrees: you are lost.

Would you resign?

<!--step:wrong-outcome-->

I didn't.

Later on, it was my opponent who resigned.

<!--step:wrong-guess-->

Here are 100 real games. In each, a player at my level lost their queen in an even position, and played on.

How many of them do you think did not lose?

<!--step:wrong-play-->

13 did not lose.

<!--step:wrong-saved-->

Not one of them came back alone. Every time, the other player went wrong somewhere: a careless move, a clock running down, a stalemate.

And every time, someone was still there to take it. About half won their way back on the board; the others were still far behind when the other player slipped.

<!--step:wrong-payoff-->

Resigning costs about 8 rating points, every time. Playing on costs the same 8 points at worst, because by now the loss is already there.

<!--step:wrong-taleb-->

Everything above that line is a chance. The writer Nassim Taleb calls this optionality: a small, known downside, and an upside you keep for free.

In chess the upside is small too, a draw or a win. But staying costs only time: about 13 more moves.

<!--step:wrong-clock-->

One thing changes the odds more than anything else: the other player's clock.

From 16 in 100 to 51 in 100, for players under 800.

<!--step:wrong-levels-->

The stronger the player, the more often they resign: half of them above 2000.

Yet those strong players who stay are the most likely to be saved: 28%, against 13% at 1000–1199.

<!--step:wrong-mine-->

I lost my queen like this 108 times and stayed 102 times. I saved 14 games; players in exactly my situations would have saved 15.

So I am not better at coming back. I just almost always stay.

But it can get worse than losing the queen.

<!--step:you-queens-->

<!--step:wrong-king-->

Another time, I was left with only my king, and three pawns were walking towards the end of the board.

<!--step:wrong-outcome-king-->

I didn't.

All three pawns became queens. Eighteen moves later, my king had nowhere left to go: stalemate.

<!--step:wrong-guess-king-->

Here are 100 real games. In each, a player at my level (rated 600–799) was left with only their king, and the other side could still checkmate. They played on.

How many of them do you think did not lose?

<!--step:wrong-play-king-->

27 did not lose. None won: a king alone cannot. 14 were stalemates; the others were draws when the other clock ran out, or when the king took the last pieces.

Every 100 games played on cost 549 rating points. Resigning them all would have cost 766.

<!--step:wrong-payoff-king-->

The same price as before: resigning costs 8 points, staying costs at most the same 8. But a king alone cannot win, so the only upside is a draw. 27 in 100 still found one, for about 8 more moves.

<!--step:wrong-mine-king-->

In rapid, only my king was left 260 times. I gave up 5 times. 62 times I did not lose, 43 of them by stalemate.

<!--step:wrong-meet-->

A queen down, 15 in 100 players who stayed did not lose. A king alone, 27 in 100.

The worse it looked, the more often staying saved the game.

<!--step:wrong-you-->

I never resign. Not out of overconfidence (if anything, I'm the opposite), but because I believe in uncertainty, and in the beauty of it.

A queen down, I played on 102 times out of 108. I saved 14.

### What our *choices* reveal

I was playing the safest opening I knew, and against my own nature.

He still saw it.

Can you hide who you are at the board? First I needed a way to describe how someone plays.

<!--step:choose-contact-->

**Contact.**\
How often a move forces something: a check, a capture, a threat. Builders rarely; attackers often.

Each grey dot is a player rated 600–900. I'm more forcing than 93 in 100.

<!--step:choose-pace-->

**Pace.**\
How often a move is made in under two seconds. Deep thinkers rarely; blitz thinkers often.

Whatever I say about fast chess, I play fast: quicker than 85 in 100.

<!--step:choose-persistence-->

**Persistence.**\
Of the games lost, how many are played to the end instead of resigned.

I keep going, more than 99 in 100.

<!--step:choose-habit-->

**Habit.**\
How often the same first three moves come back. Explorers vary; creatures of habit repeat.

I repeat myself more than 87 in 100.

<!--step:choose-all-->

The shaded bands hold the middle half of players at my level.

I'm outside them on all four: further from typical than 96 in 100.

### Method and data

- **Games.** Chess.com's public data: my 2,007 games (to 15 November 2026), and the games of a random sample of 12,000 players, July to September 2026 (4.65 million). No other player is named; the strangers of Part II and how I picture them are invented.
- **Time in games.** Minutes a day from each game's recorded start and end, smoothed over about ten days. The social media line is my own rough estimate, not a measurement.
- **Rapid and bullet.** July–September median ratings of 1,388 players with at least 20 games in each.
- **Pace in rapid.** My 267 ten-minute games in July–September, compared with 1,252 players within 100 points of my median rapid rating, each with at least 20 such games. For each game lasting at least 10 moves, seconds used divided by moves played; then the median across games for each player. My 5.0 seconds against a peer median of 7.7; faster than 92.6% of peers, rounded to 93%.
- **Usernames.** Names sorted into groups by 47 hand-checked rules; each group's ratings set against a typical opponent's, with 95% ranges from resampling.
- **Losing the queen.** Every rapid game of the sample (and 1 in 5 blitz and bullet games): the first queen lost from an even position, counted by material, 389,170 cases; a Stockfish 19 check (depth 12) on 1 in 45 found 96% of them lost. Rating points are Chess.com's usual ±8 between equal ratings, adjusted for the rating gap. My own games (and my opponents') are counted by the same rules: rapid, to 15 November.
- **Only the king left.** All 4.65 million games: 337,234 in which one side had only its king left and the other could still checkmate. My own: rapid games.
- **The four dimensions.** Ten-minute rapid games of at least 20 half-moves. Contact: the share of moves 5 to 25 that check, capture or directly threaten a piece. Pace: the share of moves from move 8 made in under 2 seconds with at least a minute on the clock. Persistence: the share of lost games not ended by resigning or leaving. Habit: the share of games in a player's most common first three moves, per colour, in draws of 30 games. Mine are from my games since 23 March 2026, against the 1,635 sample players rated 600–900 with at least 30 such games.
- **The game.** 5,160 sample players with at least 60 ten-minute games: 30 at random describe them, the mystery games come from the other 30. Ten players within 75 rating points; the computer picks the smallest distance across the four dimensions, each measured against all players' spread; 8 trials per player for each number of games (41,280 in all). Mine: 3,000 trials each, from my games since 23 March, and from my 157 London-months games (1 February to 23 March). The first lineup shown was chosen to show the idea; the others are drawn at random.
- **Your dimensions.** If you put your games in, the same rules run in your browser on your rated ten-minute games among your last 12 months' rapid games (up to 1,000; at least 30 needed). "Your level" is the sample players within 150 rating points of your average rating in those games (wider where players are few). "Find you" draws 30 of your games for your card and 5 others as the mystery, among nine players within 75 points, not chosen to work. Your line is 1,000 trials at each number of games, as far as your games allow (30 for the card, plus the mystery games). The port to the browser was checked against my games, game by game.
