## Looking for answers in the data

<!--step:data-intro-->

Each of these questions started at the board, not in a spreadsheet.

<!--step:say-message-->

### What we say about ourselves

“I would be soooo bad at blitz.”

<!--step:say-isolation-->

If I only looked at my own ratings, the verdict seemed obvious.

Here was rapid: the game I knew. And here, on 18 May, the claim.

<!--step:say-prediction-->

Seven weeks later, I tried bullet. 309 games later, its rating had settled in the 400s, while rapid kept climbing.

I said I would be bad. From where I stood, it looked as though I was right.

<!--step:say-widen-->

But lower than what? Lower than whom?

So I stopped comparing me to me, and started comparing me to everyone else.

<!--step:say-crowd-->

Each dot is a player who plays both. Rapid rating across; bullet rating up.

Under the diagonal, bullet is lower than rapid. That is where **87%** of these players sit.

How much lower, typically? Make your guess under the chart.

<!--step:say-perspective-->

And there I am, close to the typical line.

The gap I had taken so personally was ordinary.

Maybe this was less about me, and more about how bullet works.

<!--step:say-guess-->

I had made a claim about myself. Plenty of players put theirs in their usernames.

Before looking at their ratings, what would you expect? Make a guess for each kind of name.

<!--step:say-surprise-->

The apologisers and the boasters both sit a little below a typical opponent. The players joking about blunders are above, and those aiming high are higher still.

<!--step:say-limits-->

A name is a claim, not a reliable guide to the person across the board. These are patterns across groups; any one player can be the exception.

People who call themselves bad aren't worse than those who call themselves good. But they do give up more often.

<!--step:wrong-observation-->

### When things go wrong

Over the board, I kept noticing the same thing: an opponent loses their queen, and a few seconds later, they resign.

Of 107 opponents who lost their queen against me, 62 resigned. 38 of them straight away.

<!--step:wrong-decide-->

Now it's you. Move 9, playing White. Your queen is gone, and the engine agrees: you are lost.

Would you resign?

<!--step:wrong-outcome-->

I didn't.

Thirty-seven moves later, it was my opponent who resigned.

<!--step:wrong-crowd-->

Across 6,625 rapid games where someone lost their queen in a position the engine calls lost, this is what players did.

<!--step:wrong-right-->

The higher the rating, the more players resign, and the more often they are right. Above 1500, only 1 in 10 of those who play on is saved.

Down where I play, it's about 1 in 5.

<!--step:wrong-you-->

I never resign. Not out of overconfidence (if anything, I'm the opposite), but because I believe in uncertainty, and in the beauty of it.

A queen down, I played on in 119 games. I saved 15.

<!--step:wrong-stalemate-->

A game can always end in a stalemate, and that is a better outcome than losing. 58 of mine did.

This is one of them: my lone king against three queens.

<!--step:reveal-professor-->

### What our choices reveal

He said it in early 2026, while I was still playing the London System.

On 23 March, I stopped. I haven't played it since.

<!--step:reveal-guess-->

You've seen a little of how I play. Where would you place me?

<!--step:reveal-initiative-->

**Initiative.** How often my moves force something: a check, a capture, a threat.

During the London months, I was ordinary. Since the switch, my moves are more forcing than those of 93 in 100 players at my rating.

<!--step:reveal-safety-->

**Safety.** How early I castle.

Careful then, careful now. And I do trade queens more often than not, earlier than almost anyone at my level.

<!--step:reveal-tempo-->

**Tempo.** How often I move in under two seconds.

In life I'm impulsive, and on the board I sometimes move fast without thinking too much. Since the switch, faster than 86 in 100 players.

Oddly, my fast moves are rarely my mistakes.

<!--step:reveal-verdict-->

So he was right. Just not yet.

When he said it, my games didn't show it. A few weeks later, they did.

<!--step:reveal-limits-->

Maybe he saw something in me. Maybe his question changed how I played. The games can't tell which.

And the scales describe how I play, not who I am.
